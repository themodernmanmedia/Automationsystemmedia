## 3. The content types (ranked by recent performance)

1. **Gulf / migrant-worker survival testimony**: by far the strongest. A young East African
   woman takes a "housemaid" job in Saudi Arabia, Oman or elsewhere in the Middle East, is
   abused or "sold", and escapes. These are the two biggest hits (882K and 502K), and similar
   videos cluster in the 90–140K range. Variants include trafficking to Russia, Thailand or
   Nigeria.
2. **Love and relationship regret confessions**: "I regret being a second wife", "I regret
   marrying an old man", "my Arab husband…". Usually 70–200K.
3. **Unusual love stories**: age gaps, size or colour differences, a foreigner who moves to an
   African village. Feel-good episodes that do well (80–199K).
4. **Crime confessions**: "I killed…", "I stole the President's money…". Mixed results,
   15–144K. The higher the personal stakes, the better.
5. **Tribe and village life**: Hadza (190K), the village with no toilets (17K). Strong when the
   hook is "unchanged for 50,000 years", weak when the hook is just squalor.
6. **Wonder and science**: the flying car, AI reading a lost language. Mid-range.
7. **New experiments**: moral stories and Blender animation. Currently weak (8–18K).

## 4. The formula (inferred from titles plus the genre the channel built)

**Title grammar:** almost always a **first-person quote from the subject**, in plain spoken
English, with a twist in the second clause:

> `I [did/suffered extreme thing] – [twist / cost / lesson]`
> - "I Risked My Son's Life to Escape Saudi Arabia – **Was It Really Worth It?**"
> - "I Was Sold Like a Goat in Arabia, **They Trade Human Beings Like Animals**"
> - "I Regret Marrying an Old Man, I Paid Heavily – **Young Girls, Please Listen**"

Patterns that recur:
- The subject's own voice, "I…", rather than a narrator's "this woman…".
- A concrete, visceral image: *sold like a goat*, *called me a monkey*, *inside the helicopter*.
- A **second clause** that opens a loop ("Was it worth it?", "But that was just the beginning")
  or speaks to the viewer ("Young girls, please listen").
- Country names as search and emotion anchors: Saudi Arabia, Arabia, Russia, Nigeria, Thailand.

**Episode structure (genre standard for sit-down African testimony channels):**
1. **Cold open (0:00–0:45)**: the most shocking 2–4 lines of the interview, cut back to back
   over sad piano or strings, sometimes with title text.
2. **Introduction**: "My name is ___, I'm ___ years old, I come from ___." Then home
   environment B-roll: village, family, the house.
3. **Life before**: poverty and responsibility (children, sick parent, school fees). This makes
   the later choice understandable rather than foolish.
4. **The offer**: an agent, a friend, a "good job" promise, specific money figures.
5. **Arrival and the turn**: passport taken, the first shock, the moment they realised.
6. **Escalation**: two or three specific, sensory scenes of abuse or hardship. Never summarised;
   always told as a scene.
7. **Lowest point**: the near-death or "I wanted to give up" moment.
8. **The escape or turn**: who helped and how. Real tension, step by step.
9. **Coming home / now**: today's life, scars, the child, the business.
10. **Message to viewers**: direct to camera. "To the young girls who want to go…"
11. **Outro / CTA**: how to help or contact the subject, subscribe, and share so others don't
    fall into the same trap.

**Interview style:** an off-camera interviewer asks short, plain questions ("What happened
next?", "How did you feel?", "Where was your passport?"). Questions are often cut out so the
subject seems to speak continuously, and some are kept on screen as text. The subject speaks
English, sometimes in a local language with subtitles.

**Visual grammar:** a single medium shot of the subject (often outdoors or in their home, in
natural light), cut with B-roll of the village, their hands, children, documents and photos from
abroad, and walking shots. Emotional music bed throughout. Lower-thirds for names and places.

**Thumbnail grammar:** a close-up of the subject's face showing emotion, 2–5 words of bold
caption, often a country flag or a contrasting inset image (the employer's house, a passport).

## 5. Why this works

- **Search plus browse:** "Saudi Arabia housemaid", "trafficked", "escape" are terms with
  steady search demand from East Africa and the diaspora. Browse traffic comes from the emotional
  title and face.
- **Prevention value:** viewers share these with sisters and daughters who are considering Gulf
  jobs. The "please listen" line turns entertainment into a warning, and that drives shares.
- **Real, documented problem:** abuse of African domestic workers under the Gulf *kafala*
  sponsorship system is widely reported by the IOM, Human Rights Watch, Amnesty and East African
  newspapers. The stories land because viewers know they are true to life.

## 6. Ethics and production rules (important if you copy this format)

- **Use real people only with informed, written consent.** Offer to blur or shade faces and
  change names. Many survivors face stigma or retaliation.
- **Never present a scripted or AI-generated person as a real survivor.** If you dramatise,
  label it on screen: "Dramatisation — based on real accounts".
- **Do not monetise someone's trauma without giving back**, for example through a share of
  revenue, a fundraiser link, or a referral to support organisations.
- **Do not name or show identifiable employers or agents** without legal review (defamation).
- **Fact-check context lines** such as statistics and laws against current IOM, ILO or HRW sources
  before publishing.
