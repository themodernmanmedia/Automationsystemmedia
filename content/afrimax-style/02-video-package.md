# Episode Package: "He Told Me 'I Bought You' – The Day I Learned Why I Was Sent to Oman"

**Format:** first-person survivor testimony documentary (the format behind Afrimax's 882K and
502K hits).
**Runtime target:** about 20 minutes. The spoken copy is about 2,900 words at a slow, emotional
pace of roughly 140 wpm, plus pauses and B-roll breathing room.
**Language:** English, with local-language lines (Luganda) subtitled.

> ### ⚠️ Read first: how to use this script honestly
> "Nakato" is a **composite, fictional character**. Her story is built from the patterns
> widely reported about East African domestic workers in the Gulf: recruitment-agent debt,
> passport confiscation, unpaid wages, confinement, and escape through community networks.
> You have two honest ways to produce it:
> 1. **Real subject (recommended):** use this as your **interview guide and edit blueprint**.
>    Find a real returnee through a support organisation, get written consent, ask the
>    questions in the `QUESTION` lines, and cut her real answers into this structure. Her
>    real words replace the scripted ones.
> 2. **Dramatisation:** cast an actor and keep the on-screen label **"Dramatisation — based
>    on real accounts"** for the whole video, plus a line in the description.
>
> Do **not** publish it with an actor or an AI voice or avatar presented as a real survivor.

## C. Full script

### SCENE 1: COLD OPEN (00:00–00:48)

`MUSIC:` Single low piano note, sustained. Silence underneath.

`VISUAL:` Black. Then a slow push-in on Nakato's close-up. She isn't speaking yet. She looks
down at her hands.

`NAKATO:`
> The first night, he took my passport. I asked him, "Sir, when will you give it back?"
> He laughed. He said, "Give it back? I bought you. I paid three thousand dollars for you."

`VISUAL:` Hard cut. A cold-graded shot of a white villa gate closing, and the metal latch.

`SFX:` Gate clang, with a reverb tail.

`NAKATO:` *(wide shot, under the tree)*
> I didn't know that a human being could be bought. I thought slavery ended a long time ago.

`VISUAL:` Faceless reconstruction: a bathroom window at night, a hand gripping the frame.

`NAKATO:` *(close-up, voice breaking)*
> Fourteen months. I never saw the street. Not one time.

`NAKATO:` *(tiny pause, looks to the lens)*
> If I had stayed one more week in that house, I would not be here talking to you.

`MUSIC:` Strings swell, then cut.

`TEXT:` (centre, white, typewriter reveal)
**Masaka, Uganda.**
**Nakato was promised a supermarket job in Oman.**
**She was never allowed to leave the house.**

`TEXT (if dramatised, bottom right, stays all video):` *Dramatisation — based on real accounts*

### SCENE 2: WHO IS NAKATO? (00:48–02:30)

`MUSIC:` Gentle acoustic guitar, warm.

`VISUAL:` Drone or high-angle shot over the banana plantation and red-earth road. Children
running with a tyre. Nakato's mother sorting beans on a mat. Two small boys, about 6 and 9,
kicking a ball made of plastic bags.

`TEXT:` (lower-third) **NAKATO, 28 — Masaka District, Uganda**

`QUESTION:` Tell us who you are.

`NAKATO:`
> My name is Nakato. I'm twenty-eight years old. I was born here, in this village, near Masaka.
> These are my two sons, Jonah and Elijah. That one, the small one, he was four when I left.
> When I came back, he didn't know me. He hid behind my mother.

`VISUAL:` The younger boy peeks from behind his grandmother's dress, shy.

`NAKATO:`
> My father died when I was in Senior Three, so I left school. I started selling tomatoes and
> silver fish in the market. Then I got married, I had my children, and… the man left. He went
> to Kampala and never came back.

`QUESTION:` How was life before you travelled?

`NAKATO:`
> Very hard. You wake up at five, you go to the market, by evening you have maybe eight thousand
> shillings. That's two dollars. School fees for two children… my mother's medicine for her
> pressure… you cannot. Sometimes we ate one meal. Posho and beans, that's it.

`VISUAL:` Close on a cooking pot over three stones. Smoke. Her hands peeling matooke.

`NAKATO:`
> I was not looking to be rich. I just wanted my children to stay in school. That's all I wanted.

---

### SCENE 3: THE OFFER THAT LOOKED LIKE A MIRACLE (02:30–05:10)

`MUSIC:` Light, hopeful pulse: marimba and soft pad.

`VISUAL:` B-roll of a Kampala taxi park and busy Kikuubo street. A faceless shot of a hand
holding a printed flyer ("JOBS IN THE MIDDLE EAST — SUPERMARKET, HOTEL, CLEANING —
SALARY $400").

`QUESTION:` How did you hear about the job?

`NAKATO:`
> A woman from our church, she had a cousin who was working in Dubai. She sent money, her family
> built a permanent house. Everybody was talking about it. So she gave me the number of an agent
> in Kampala.

> I went to the office. It was nice, air conditioning, there were certificates on the wall. The man
> was very polite. He told me: "Nakato, you are lucky. We have a supermarket in Muscat, Oman. You
> will be arranging shelves, four hundred dollars a month, accommodation free, food free, you get
> one day off every week."

`TEXT:` **$400 a month ≈ 1.5 million Uganda shillings**

`NAKATO:` *(smiles slightly, shakes her head at her younger self)*
> Four hundred dollars. In my mind I was already calculating: school fees for one year in two
> months. I could not sleep that night.

`QUESTION:` Did you pay anything?

`NAKATO:`
> Yes. They said medical, passport, "processing", training… two million eight hundred thousand
> shillings. I didn't have it. My mother had a small piece of land, near the swamp. We sold half.

`VISUAL:` Nakato's mother, Jaja Namubiru, sitting on a wooden stool.

`TEXT:` (lower-third) **NAMUBIRU — Nakato's mother**

`NAMUBIRU:` *(in Luganda, subtitled)*
> *I told her, "My daughter, that land was your father's." But she cried, she said "Mama, I will
> buy it back double." What can a mother say? I signed.*

`QUESTION:` Did you sign a contract?

`NAKATO:`
> They gave me papers on the last day, in the office, in a hurry. It was in English and Arabic. The
> agent said, "Just sign here, here, here, the bus is waiting." I signed. I never got a copy.

`VISUAL:` Faceless reconstruction: a pen signing quickly, pages flipped before they can be
read. Slow motion.

`MUSIC:` Pulse drops out, leaving a single held note.

`NAKATO:`
> That was the first mistake. If you remember one thing from me, remember that one. Never sign
> what you didn't read.

---

### SCENE 4: ARRIVAL IN MUSCAT (05:10–07:40)

`MUSIC:` Cold drone, airport ambience.

`VISUAL:` Stock: a plane taking off at night, then airport corridors. Colour shifts to cold
teal-grey. Text slides on.

`TEXT:` **Entebbe → Muscat. March.**

`QUESTION:` What happened when you landed?

`NAKATO:`
> We were six girls on that flight. Two from Masaka, others from Jinja, Mbale. At the airport a man
> was waiting with our names on a paper. He took us to an office, very small. They took our phones
> "for registration".

> Then people started coming. Omani men, women in black. They looked at us… like… *(pauses)* like
> you look at tomatoes in the market. Turning, checking. One woman opened my mouth to see my teeth.

`VISUAL:` Hold on her close-up. She presses her lips together. Three seconds of silence, no
music.

`NAKATO:`
> I asked the girl next to me, "Where is the supermarket?" She was quiet. She was crying.

`QUESTION:` Who took you?

`NAKATO:`
> A man, maybe fifty years. White *dishdasha*. He signed papers, gave money to the office man, a lot of
> notes. I saw it. Then he said, "Come." I followed.

`VISUAL:` Reconstruction: night, the back seat of an SUV, city lights smearing past the window.
Only her hands in her lap are visible.

`NAKATO:`
> We drove maybe one hour. A big white house, a high wall, a gate. Inside, he said, "Passport."
> I gave him. I thought it was for the company. Then I asked, "Sir, when will you return it?"

`SFX:` A single deep piano note.

`NAKATO:`
> He laughed. He said, "Return? I bought you. Three thousand dollars. You work, you finish my
> money, then we talk."

`TEXT:` (white on black, full screen, 3 seconds)
**"I bought you."**

`NAKATO:` *(quiet)*
> That night I didn't sleep. I was in a small room near the kitchen. No window. I was just
> listening to the air conditioner, thinking about my children.

---

### SCENE 5: INSIDE THE HOUSE (07:40–11:20)

`MUSIC:` Slow ticking clock pulse under low cello.

`VISUAL:` Faceless reconstruction montage: a bucket and mop on white marble, hands raw from
detergent, feet climbing a curved staircase, laundry piled on a bed, a kitchen clock reading
2:14 a.m.

`QUESTION:` Describe a normal day.

`NAKATO:`
> There is no normal day. I wake at five. Prepare breakfast for the family. There were eleven
> people: the man, his wife, his mother, and eight children. Then I clean the whole house. Three
> floors. Every day you wash every floor, the stairs, the toilets, seven toilets.

> Then lunch. Then ironing. Then dinner. Then they have visitors at night, and I serve tea until
> one, two in the morning. Then I wash the dishes. I would sleep maybe three hours, four hours.

`TEXT:` **19–20 hours of work a day. No day off.**

`QUESTION:` Were you paid?

`NAKATO:`
> The first month, nothing. I asked. Madam said, "Your salary goes to pay back what we spent on you."
> The second month, nothing. The third month, she gave me fifty riyals and said, "Send to your family."

`TEXT:` **50 Omani rials ≈ $130 — for three months of work**

`NAKATO:`
> I was supposed to get four hundred dollars every month. And I wasn't arranging shelves in any
> supermarket. I was a housemaid. Nobody told me.

`QUESTION:` Could you talk to your family?

`NAKATO:`
> They gave back my phone after two months, but only for ten minutes on Friday, with Madam
> standing there. I could not say anything. My mother would ask, "Are you okay?" I say, "Yes, Mama,
> I am fine, the work is good." Then I go to the bathroom and cry with the tap open so nobody
> hears.

`VISUAL:` Reconstruction: a running tap, close up, water swirling down a white sink.

`QUESTION:` How did they treat you?

`NAKATO:` *(long pause; she looks away from camera)*
> Madam would slap me if the food was late, or if a child cried. They called me *"hayawan"*. It
> means animal. The small children also learned it. A child of seven, telling me, "Hayawan, bring
> water."

> The food… I ate what remained on the plates. If nothing remained, I ate bread. I lost so much
> weight. When I came back, my mother didn't know me at the airport.

`VISUAL:` A still photo held in Nakato's hand: her before travel, fuller-faced and smiling at
a wedding. Slow push-in.

`NAKATO:`
> And the man… *(pauses, hands tighten)* …when Madam went to visit her sister, he started coming to
> the kitchen at night. I would lock my room with a chair. He banged the door. I pushed the chair
> with my back the whole night.

`MUSIC:` Cello stops. Room tone only.

`NAKATO:`
> I'm not going to say everything. Some things I keep. But every girl who has been to those houses
> will understand what I mean.

`VISUAL:` Hold on her face for 4 seconds. Fade to black for 1 second.

---

### SCENE 6: THE NIGHT SHE ALMOST GAVE UP (11:20–13:15)

`MUSIC:` A sparse, aching solo piano.

`VISUAL:` Night in the village today: a kerosene lamp, crickets. Nakato sitting alone on the
step of her mother's house.

`QUESTION:` Was there a moment you wanted to give up?

`NAKATO:`
> Month eleven. My mother called… she was sick, they took her to Masaka hospital. My son Elijah
> was sent home from school for fees. And I had nothing. Fifty riyals from three months, I already
> sent it.

> That night I went to the roof, where we hang clothes. It is high, three floors. I stood there.
> I was thinking, "If I jump, at least this finishes."

`VISUAL:` Reconstruction: washing lines on a rooftop at night, sheets moving in the wind.
City glow in the distance. No person in frame.

`NAKATO:` *(tears; she wipes them with the back of her hand)*
> Then I heard the call to prayer from the mosque, very loud. And I remembered my son, the small
> one, how he holds my finger when he sleeps. I said, "God, if you want me to live, show me a way."
> And I went back down.

`MUSIC:` A single warm chord enters, the first in the film.

`NAKATO:`
> Two days later, God showed me the way.

---

### SCENE 7: THE BATHROOM WINDOW (13:15–16:20)

`MUSIC:` A tension pulse builds slowly: heartbeat kick, rising strings.

`QUESTION:` What happened?

`NAKATO:`
> There was a Kenyan girl working in the house next door, Wanjiru. We would see each other
> sometimes when we put out the rubbish. We could only talk for one minute. One day she put a small
> paper in my hand. A phone number. She said, "These are Ugandan women here in Oman. They help
> girls like us. Save it in your head, not in your phone."

`TEXT:` **Many Gulf escapes happen through informal community networks of other migrant
workers.**

`NAKATO:`
> I memorised it. That Friday, when Madam gave me the phone, she went to answer the door. I had
> maybe thirty seconds. I sent a WhatsApp to that number: "My name is Nakato from Uganda, they
> took my passport, help me." And the house location. Then I deleted everything.

`VISUAL:` Reconstruction: a thumb typing fast on a cracked screen, the "Location shared"
bubble, the thumb holding Delete. A door handle turning in the background, out of focus.

`SFX:` A door handle click. Footsteps.

`NAKATO:`
> They replied the next week, when I had the phone again. A lady. She said, "Don't be afraid. On
> Thursday night the family goes to a wedding. Be ready. We will park on the back street.
> White car. Flash the lights two times."

`QUESTION:` How did you get out?

`NAKATO:`
> The gate was locked. The doors were locked, and they took the keys. Only my bathroom, the small
> one for the maid, had a window. Small, like this. *(she shows a gap with her hands)* High on the
> wall, with a net for mosquitoes.

> Thursday, they left at nine. I waited thirty minutes in case they came back for something. Then I
> stood on the toilet, I tore the net, and I pushed. My shoulders could not pass. I took off my
> dress. Only in my underwear, I pushed again, my skin was tearing on the metal. I was bleeding
> here *(touches her upper arm)*. You can still see the mark.

`VISUAL:` Close-up today: a pale scar on her upper arm. She turns her arm slowly toward the
light.

`NAKATO:`
> I fell outside onto the ground behind the house, between the wall and the generator. Then there was
> still the outside wall, very high. But near the generator there was a water tank. I climbed the
> tank, and from the tank to the wall. I didn't think. I just jumped.

`SFX:` A heavy thud, then silence. Dogs barking in the distance.

`NAKATO:`
> My ankle twisted. I was lying on the street, no dress, no shoes, no passport. And then I saw it:
> a white car, the lights went on and off, two times.

`MUSIC:` Strings resolve into a long, warm sustained chord.

`NAKATO:` *(crying and smiling at the same time)*
> The lady jumped out with a *kitenge* cloth and wrapped me. She was speaking Luganda. She said,
> *"Tuli wamu, mwana wange."* "We are together, my child." When I heard Luganda… I can't explain.
> I felt like I was home already.

`TEXT:` (subtitle) *"Tuli wamu, mwana wange" — "We are together, my child."*

### SCENE 8: COMING HOME WITH NOTHING (16:20–18:10)

`MUSIC:` Gentle acoustic guitar returns.

`QUESTION:` What happened after?

`NAKATO:`
> They hid me for some days in a room with other girls, five of us, all running from houses.
> They took me to the police to make a statement. It was not easy. My employer reported me as
> "absconding", like I am the criminal. That is how the system works. You run away, you become the
> one in trouble.

`TEXT:` **Under the *kafala* sponsorship system, a worker's legal status is tied to her
employer. Workers who flee can be reported as "absconding".**
*(Fact-check this line against current ILO, HRW or IOM sources before publishing. Oman has
announced labour reforms in recent years.)*

`NAKATO:`
> It took almost two months. The community contributed money, and an organisation helped with the
> emergency travel document and the ticket. Then I was on the plane.

`VISUAL:` Stock: a plane landing at Entebbe in daylight. Colour shifts back to warm. Then Nakato
today, walking up the red-earth path toward her mother's house, a small bag on her shoulder.

`NAKATO:`
> I came back with nothing. No money, no passport, nothing in my hands. Fourteen months. My
> mother's land, gone. People in the village were saying, "She went to Arabia and came back
> empty, maybe she was doing bad things there." That hurt me more than the slaps.

`QUESTION:` How is your life now?

`NAKATO:`
> Now I'm back in the market. Tomatoes and silver fish, like before. *(small laugh)* But I'm with my
> children. Jonah is in Primary Six. The small one, Elijah, now he doesn't leave me alone. He
> follows me even to the toilet. *(laughs, wipes eyes)* He's scared I will disappear again.

`VISUAL:` Nakato at her market stall, arranging tomatoes in neat pyramids. Elijah sitting on
an upturned crate beside her, eating a sugarcane stick. Then the boys walking home from school
in uniform.

### SCENE 9: HER MESSAGE TO YOUNG GIRLS (18:10–19:30)

`MUSIC:` Soft piano, a hopeful variation of the opening theme.

`VISUAL:` Back to the main close-up. She sits up straighter and looks **directly into the lens**
for the first time.

`QUESTION:` What would you tell a girl who is planning to go?

`NAKATO:`
> To the young girls watching, my sisters: I'm not telling you never go. Some people go and they
> are okay. But please, please listen to me.

> One: check if the agency is registered with the Ministry of Labour. Ask for the licence. If they
> refuse, run.

> Two: never, ever give your passport to anyone. It's your life in that small book.

> Three: before you go, save the number of the embassy, and give your family the name and address
> of the employer. If they don't want to tell you where you are going, that's a sign.

> And four… *(pauses)* if they promise you a supermarket, ask them to show you the supermarket.

`VISUAL:` Her slight, knowing smile. Hold.

`NAKATO:`
> I sold my mother's land for a dream. I don't want any other mother to sell her land for nothing.

`MUSIC:` Resolves.

## D. Interview question guide (for a real subject)

Ask in this order and let silence do the work: wait 3–5 seconds after each answer before the
next question.

1. What is your name, your age, and where are you from?
2. Who is in your family? Who depends on you?
3. What was a normal day like before you travelled? How much were you earning?
4. Who told you about the job? What exactly did they promise: job, salary, conditions?
5. How much did you pay, and where did that money come from?
6. Did you sign anything? Did you read it? Did you get a copy?
7. Describe the day you landed, from the airport to the house, step by step.
8. What happened to your passport and your phone?
9. Describe one ordinary day in the house, hour by hour.
10. Were you paid? How much, and when?
11. How did they speak to you? What did they call you?
12. Is there a moment you think about every day? *(Do not push. Offer to stop or skip.)*
13. Was there a time you wanted to give up? What kept you going?
14. How did you get out? Who helped? Walk me through that night.
15. What happened with the police and the papers, and how did you get home?
16. How did people treat you when you came back?
17. What does your life look like today?
18. Look into the camera. What would you tell a girl who is planning to go?

**Safeguarding:** have a counsellor's or support organisation's number ready. Tell the
subject she can stop at any time, can withdraw consent before publishing, and can choose a
blurred face or an altered voice. Never film or name the employer, the agent or the people who
helped her escape.
